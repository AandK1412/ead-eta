What USCIS says vs. what its backlog implies

Two readings of the same queue, from the same agency, in the same quarter. They disagree — and the gap is the story.

What each bar actually measures
  • Published processing time — the median of cases USCIS completed this quarter. It describes finished cases, so it systematically under-represents anyone still stuck in the queue.
  • Backlog-implied wait — pending ÷ quarterly completions (Little's Law). A mean time in system for everything currently queued. Adjudication isn't strictly FIFO and long-stuck cases inflate it, so read it as queue depth — an upper bound, not a median.
  • You, so far — days since your receipt date.

The first is a median and the second a mean of a right-skewed distribution, so they are not strictly like-for-like. Compare direction and magnitude, not decimals.

Neither bar is the answer — the answer is between them. Independent reporting for February 2026 put about 1.78 million I-765 cases pending with roughly 1.18 million of them (66%) waiting more than six months, against a published average of 4.8 months. Those two facts can't describe the same population: if the typical wait really were under five months, two-thirds of the queue couldn't already be past six. But the backlog-implied figure overshoots in the other direction, running about 3× the reported average. Best current reading for the aggregate is somewhere in the 6–12 month range.     Method & honest limits

    Every figure here comes from two USCIS publications: the quarterly All USCIS Application and Petition Form Types report and the Net Backlog and Frontlog report, plus the Historical Processing Times Trends FY2016–FY2024 factsheet. Links are in the footer.

    What this can tell you

    • How your elapsed wait compares to the published median.
    • How deep the queue is for your category, in months of throughput.
    • Whether waits for your basis of filing have been rising or falling.
    • What share of pending cases are already past USCIS's own target.

    What it can't

    • No percentiles. USCIS publishes aggregate counts, not per-case durations, so there is no honest way to derive a P80 or P90 from it. Anything claiming otherwise is modelling, not measuring.
    • Coarse categories. USCIS reports four I-765 buckets. OPT, STEM OPT, H-4 and L-2 all sit inside "All other", so they share a number even though their real experiences differ.
    • National, not per-center. No service-center breakdown.
    • Quarterly lag. Figures trail real time by up to a quarter.
    • Methodology shift. The historical series uses a cycle-time method that differs from today's processing-time page. Read its shape, don't subtract it from current figures.
